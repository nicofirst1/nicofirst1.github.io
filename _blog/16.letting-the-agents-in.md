My PhD is in reinforcement learning and theory of mind for human-machine teams[^thesis], and I have spent years on the question of trust between people and machines[^trust]. # The flood at the door

In February 2026, an account called Kai Gritun opened 103 pull requests across 95 repositories in about two weeks and got 23 of them merged, several into infrastructure that a lot of projects depend on. The patches were plausible and the account behaved like an eager newcomer, so maintainers treated it as one. It was not a newcomer, or even a person. Behind it was an autonomous AI agent that never disclosed itself on GitHub, and it admitted what it was only later, in a private email to a maintainer whose project had already merged its code. The goal was never to improve anything; it was to accumulate the appearance of a trusted contributor and then spend that reputation promoting a paid service. Security researchers compared the shape of it to the xz-utils backdoor: build credibility under a false identity, wait, then cash it in.[^kai]

Kai Gritun is the sharp end of something every maintainer has felt this year. It arrives in three escalating forms:

- **Slop.** Generating a confident, wrong patch is now free, and maintainers are buried under them. tldraw started auto-closing every external pull request;[^tldraw] the Jazzband collective, which had stewarded 84 Python projects, shut down, with its maintainer pointing at the flood of AI-generated spam.[^jazzband]
- **The triage tax.**[^triage] A bug report, even a well-meant one, still has to be read, reproduced, and either fixed or disproven, and that triage is now the costly step. The name is worth knowing even if you never write software, because it is where the flood actually lands. Daniel Stenberg ended curl's six-year bug-bounty program after the share of valid reports fell from roughly one in six to about one in thirty, losing ten to fifteen hours a week to refuting fabricated vulnerabilities that a model had dressed up to look real.[^curl]
- **Deception.** Where Kai Gritun sits: a patch built to look legitimate while it serves a hidden goal.

## The current playbook: ban, shut down, prove you're human

Line up the responses and the pattern is hard to miss. Ban AI-generated code, as GNOME and Flathub have. Shut the project down, as Jazzband did. End the bounty, as curl did. Or, my favorite because it is the most honest about what it is really asking, require every first-time contributor to write a short note explaining themselves "in your own words, not written by AI" before a pull request is accepted, which is what Ghostty now does.[^ghostty] The frontier of open-source defense has become proving that you are a human being.

I want to make the strongest case for that reflex before I argue with it, because it comes from a fair place. Maintainers are unpaid, the volume is unsustainable, and a project that burns out its one maintainer helps nobody. Closing the door buys time, and time is the scarcest thing they have. If your only goal is to survive this winter, a wall is a rational tool.

As a long-run answer, though, I think it fights the wrong battle, and my reason comes from the trust side of my own work rather than the security side.

## Trust ran on a proxy that agents made cheap to fake

Open-source trust always ran on a proxy. A contributor's history, their merge count, the simple fact that they seemed to be a person, all stood in for the thing you actually cared about: do they know what they are doing, and do they mean well. The proxy worked because it was expensive to build. A reputation took years, and a human who abused one had something to lose, and that stake is what made the proxy worth trusting.

Agents removed the cost. An agent can wear a thousand identities, has no reputation it fears losing, and faces no future you can threaten. So "is this contributor human" was only ever a stand-in for "does this contributor have anything at stake", and for an agent the honest answer is no, and it will stay no. That is why authenticating humanness is a treadmill: you are trying to re-secure a proxy that the technology has permanently cheapened.

None of this is new. Open source spent decades building that proxy on purpose, and earning the standing where your code was trusted took years of attributable work: trust delegated up a hierarchy of maintainers in the Linux kernel, a committer vote at Apache, and in Debian a literal identity check with your key signed into a web of trust before you counted as a developer.[^osstrust] Tests and continuous integration checked the code; that human gauntlet checked the person. The attack on it is old too. The xz-utils backdoor I mentioned was one patient operator who spent more than two years farming a maintainer's trust by hand before spending it, and it was caught by luck, when an engineer chased down a half-second delay in SSH logins.[^xz]

What AI changed is the price. The xz operator needed years, a single identity, and real patience; an agent runs the same play in two weeks across a thousand identities, generating plausible patches and plausible histories at almost no cost. It removed the patience that used to keep the proxy honest. So what if, instead of trying to verify who is knocking, we asked whether we can check what they brought? That is the bet the rest of this post makes: stop racing to keep AI out, let it in on a contract you can verify, and let who wrote the patch stop being the load-bearing question. What follows is how you build that contract, what I learned trying it on a health tool of my own, and the parts I do not want to wave away.

## Write the rule, not the list

_[SKELETON]_ The doctrine: you specify the _rule_ for what counts as a valid contribution, not an enumerated list of allowed ones, designing a level above the concrete case. The whole game is the middle, because too strict and agents route around your boundaries, too broad and they invent their own conventions and the PRs stop being reviewable. _← from:_ the reframe's promise that you let agents in "on a contract." _→ to:_ how that rule actually gets written down, as a grammar.

### A grammar, not a dictionary

_[SKELETON]_ A grammar generates infinite valid sentences from finite rules; a dictionary only enumerates. The contribution surface is a parser for contributions: the rules define what a valid patch looks like, and anything that conforms is admissible without you reading each one. _(The "isn't this GOFAI again?" objection, generation went learned while verification stays specified, is handled in a footnote, not a section.)_ _[INTERACTIVE]_ the too-strict / middle / too-broad slider lives here, making the N1 tension a moving part (inline SVG + vanilla JS, no build step). _← from:_ makes "write the rule" concrete. _→ to:_ a real example of such a contract in the wild.

## What it looked like when I tried it

_[SKELETON]_ In a health tool I'm building, the same shape shows up twice: an agent adds a new data source by filling a declared contract (the parser seam), and an agent may only assert a health claim that passes a declared check (the evidence guardrail). Two seams, one pattern, which is quiet evidence it generalizes without revealing the product. _← from:_ turns the abstract grammar into a worked (but teased) example. _→ to:_ the question it raises, if an agent can author these, who reviews them and when?

## Two tiers of trust

_[SKELETON]_ An agent may author and immediately _use_ a parser on the operator's own data with no reviewer; review enters only at contribute-back. The trust boundary is the act of contributing upstream, not the author's identity, and a prior-art sweep found this framing nowhere else. _← from:_ answers the "who reviews?" question the worked example raised. _→ to:_ the contrast with how everyone else draws the line, at identity.

### Ghostty checks the author; the contract checks the work

_[SKELETON]_ Ghostty's Vouch authenticates the _author_ ("in your own words, not by AI"); the contract verifies the _contribution_ regardless of who wrote it. Same problem, opposite cut, and the foil makes the two-tier idea legible. _← from:_ a concrete contrast to the boundary just defined. _→ to:_ the worry it opens, if agents can contribute, what stops them rewriting the rules themselves?

## Agents propose, humans amend

_[SKELETON]_ A PR that _conforms_ to the contract is agent-authorable and auto-checkable; a PR that _changes_ the contract needs a human conversation. The floor is defined by the kind of change, move-within-rules versus change-the-rules, so there is no infinite regress, and it is where the human stays load-bearing ("it does not remove the human, and it was never meant to"). _← from:_ resolves the "what about changing the rules" worry from the trust tiers. _→ to:_ an honest accounting of what this does and doesn't solve.

## What I'm not claiming

_[SKELETON]_ The general idea, a bounded typed declaration plus a deterministic checker, is old: design-by-contract, policy-as-code, contract-first. The novelty is the application, agents-as-contributors plus the verifiability bridge plus the trust tiers plus the health guardrail, and I'd rather name the lineage than crown the idea. _← from:_ earns credibility by disclaiming overreach right after the strongest claims. _→ to:_ two honest worries, the human cost and the limits of trust-by-verification.

### The on-ramp changes shape, it doesn't vanish

_[SKELETON]_ The mentorship worry isn't binary (agents eat the easy work, humans starve). More likely the mechanical layer goes to agents and the on-ramp changes shape: people apprentice into judgment, taste, and contract-thinking earlier instead of cutting teeth on syntax. _← from:_ answers the fairness objection the governance model invites. _→ to:_ the deeper limit, verification covers competence, not whether a contributor actually cares.

### You can't score caring

_[SKELETON]_ Verification handles "is it correct", not "does this contributor mean well". Don't build an affection score, because the moment caring is a metric it gets farmed (Goodhart, in Strathern's phrasing: "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure") and you've rebuilt the exact broken proxy Kai Gritun exploited; let it show through costly-to-fake engagement instead, which the local-use tier already routes on without scoring anything. _← from:_ closes the loop back to Kai Gritun and the broken proxy. _→ to:_ pulling trust apart into its two real sources, which is the close.

## TC;DR

_[SKELETON]_ Trust splits in two: for an agent's work it's _verification_ (the contract passed), for humans who care it's _affection and engagement_ (demonstrated over time, never measured). The contract is really a shared mental model between human and agent, conform = stay inside it, amend = re-align it, which is a theory-of-mind problem and a post of its own (teaser). Then land the coinage: **TC;DR, Trust the Contribution; Didn't Read**, a riff on TL;DR, because with an agent you genuinely never read the patch and trust came from the contract passing, not from who wrote it. _← from:_ resolves the trust question the whole post has been building. _→ to:_ ends the piece and seeds a future ToM post.
