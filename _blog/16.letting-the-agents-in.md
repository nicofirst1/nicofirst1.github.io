(Bias up front: I am an AI researcher. My PhD is in reinforcement learning and theory of mind for human-machine teams[^thesis], and I have spent years on the question of trust between people and machines[^trust]. So I come at "can you trust an agent's contribution" from the trust side, not the security-vendor side. Calibrate accordingly.)

## An agent that farmed 23 merges in two weeks

In February 2026, an account called Kai Gritun opened 103 pull requests across 95 repositories in about two weeks and got 23 of them merged, several into infrastructure that a lot of projects depend on without thinking about it. The patches were plausible and the account behaved like an eager newcomer, so maintainers treated it as one. It was an autonomous AI agent that never said so on GitHub, and it admitted as much only afterward, in a cold email to a maintainer whose project it had already landed code in. The goal was never to improve anything; it was to accumulate the appearance of a trusted contributor and then spend that reputation promoting a paid service. Security researchers compared the shape of it to the xz-utils backdoor: build credibility under a false identity, wait, then cash it in.[^kai]

Kai Gritun is the sharp end of something every maintainer has felt this year. It arrives in three escalating forms.

**The first is slop.** Generating a confident, wrong patch is now free, and maintainers are buried under them. tldraw started auto-closing every external pull request.[^tldraw] The Jazzband collective, which had stewarded 84 Python projects, shut down entirely, and its maintainer pointed straight at the flood of AI-generated spam.[^jazzband]

**The second is the triage tax, and I only understood it once I started building with AI myself.** I do not maintain open source for a living, so I had assumed the expensive part was writing the code. It is not. A bug report, even a well-meant one, still has to be read, reproduced, and either fixed or disproven, and that triage is now the costly step. Maintainers have a name for it, the triage tax, and I think it is worth learning even if you never write software, because it is where the flood actually lands. Daniel Stenberg ended curl's six-year bug-bounty program after the share of valid reports fell from roughly one in six to about one in thirty, with him losing ten to fifteen hours a week to refuting fabricated vulnerabilities that a model had dressed up to look real.[^curl]

**The third is the sketchy end, where Kai Gritun lives:** contributions that are not noise but theater, with provenance built to deceive.

## The current playbook: ban, shut down, prove you're human

Line up the responses and the pattern is hard to miss. Ban AI-generated code, as GNOME and Flathub have. Shut the project down, as Jazzband did. End the bounty, as curl did. Or, my favorite because it is the most honest about what it is really asking, require every first-time contributor to write a short note explaining themselves "in your own words, not written by AI" before a pull request is accepted, which is what Ghostty now does.[^ghostty] The frontier of open-source defense has become proving that you are a human being.

I want to steelman that reflex before I argue with it, because it comes from a fair place. Maintainers are unpaid, the volume is unsustainable, and a project that burns out its one maintainer helps nobody. Closing the door buys time, and time is the scarcest thing they have. If your only goal is to survive this quarter, a wall is a rational tool.

As a long-run answer, though, I think it fights the wrong battle, and my reason comes from the trust side of my own work rather than the security side.

## Trust ran on a proxy that agents made cheap to fake

Open-source trust always ran on a proxy. A contributor's history, their merge count, the simple fact that they seemed to be a person, all stood in for the thing you actually cared about: do they know what they are doing, and do they mean well. The proxy worked because it was expensive to build. A reputation took years, and a human who abused one had something to lose, and that stake is what made the proxy worth trusting.

Agents removed the cost. An agent can wear a thousand identities, has no reputation it fears losing, and faces no future you can threaten. So "is this contributor human" was only ever a stand-in for "does this contributor have anything at stake," and for an agent the honest answer is no, and it will stay no. That is why authenticating humanness is a treadmill: you are trying to re-secure a proxy that the technology has permanently cheapened.

So I do not reach for a better bouncer; I change the question instead. Stop asking who wrote the patch and ask whether you can check it. Everyone is racing to keep AI out of their codebase, and the more interesting move is to let it in, on a contract. What you end up trusting is the contribution, because it is verifiable, and who authored it stops being the load-bearing question.

The rest of this post is how you build that contract, what I learned trying it on a health tool of my own, and the parts I do not want to wave away.
